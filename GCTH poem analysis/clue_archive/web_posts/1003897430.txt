Biggest prizes still at large after Great Canadian Treasure Hunt’s first year

More than 80,000 Canadians have joined the adventure, discovering the remarkable stories of the mining industry that helped build our nation. Now, with 241 oz. of gold still hidden across the country, the hunt is far from over.
Somewhere in Canada, a treasure chest containing 217 1-oz. gold coins is waiting to be discovered.
Four more treasures, each containing 6 oz. of pure gold, are hidden in Quebec, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and the Sudbury region of Ontario.
That’s 241 oz. of gold still waiting to be found. And somewhere out there, someone has the chance to turn a little curiosity, a love of adventure and some Canadian ingenuity into a life-changing discovery.
But as The Northern Miner‘s Great Canadian Treasure Hunt celebrates its first year, it’s clear that something bigger than gold has been uncovered.
Across the country, Canadians have been rediscovering the stories, characters and achievements of the mining industry that helped build the country.
And they’re having fun doing it.
Treasure hunt nation
When The Northern Miner launched the Great Canadian Treasure Hunt in August last year, the idea was simple: What if we could get Canadians excited about mining by turning our country’s remarkable mining history into a real-life treasure hunt?
Participants could explore that history and have the chance to make a gold discovery of their own.
The response has exceeded our expectations.
More than 80,500 Canadians have become active treasure hunters, following clues, studying maps, watching videos and learning about communities and discoveries they may never have heard of before.
The Hunt has connected with millions across its website and social media platforms, while hundreds of news reports, television appearances and radio interviews have carried the excitement into communities from coast to coast.
Perhaps the most rewarding part has been seeing Canadians connect with the adventure — and with one another.
There were brothers Jason and Matthew Schwarz, separated by 4,000 km, who spent months working together to solve clues before discovering 6 oz. of gold in British Columbia’s legendary Golden Triangle.
For the brothers, the real treasure may have been the time spent reconnecting.
Then there were young geology graduates Oliver Baker and Bella Ruppert, who tracked down a treasure near Bathurst, N.B., only to nearly walk away because they weren’t convinced the disguised box was what they were looking for.
Fortunately, curiosity got the better of them. They returned, checked again and struck gold.
These are the stories that have made the Hunt special: Canadians embracing adventure, exploring their country and occasionally walking away considerably richer.
Mining’s next generation
Of course, the Great Canadian Treasure Hunt has always been about more than gold.
Canada is one of the world’s great mining nations. From the gold rushes that opened the West to the nickel discoveries that helped industrialize the country, mining has created communities, supported generations of families and helped build the prosperity Canadians enjoy today.
And its importance is only growing.
Speaking at Canada House in London in October, energy and natural resources Minister Tim Hodgson, declared that the world is entering the “largest investment supercycle in the history of the metals and mining industry.”
He explained that critical minerals are no longer simply industrial commodities, but essential ingredients that will determine which countries can grow, compete and secure their economic futures.
It’s a powerful reminder that the minerals beneath Canadian soil aren’t just part of our history. They’re central to our future.
Copper that carries electricity, nickel used in advanced technologies, uranium that fuels nuclear reactors and potash that helps feed the world are all part of Canada’s mineral wealth.
And that’s a story worth sharing with young Canadians who may never have considered a career in mining.
The early signs are encouraging.
Seventeen per cent of the Hunt’s social media audience is 24 or younger, far above the proportion traditionally following mining news.
Over the past year, MINING.com Jobs has recorded more than one million job views, an increase of 283%, while applications have risen 23%. Although those gains cannot be attributed solely to the Hunt, they are encouraging signs of growing interest in mining careers.
The adventure has also inspired the Junior Explorers Challenge, developed with Mining Matters, giving children aged nine to 17 an opportunity to explore Canada’s geology and mining history through puzzles, quizzes and creative challenges.
Today’s curious young treasure hunter could become tomorrow’s geologist, engineer or mining entrepreneur.
That would give the adventure a lasting impact.

Quebec: Gold, grit, the road north
Nested deep in northwestern Quebec lies one of Canada’s greatest gold-producing regions, where determination, ambition and a little prospecting luck transformed wilderness into thriving communities.
Exploring our Quebec treasure story takes us to Val-d’Or, whose name means valley of gold.
20th-century hardships during the Great Depression, when jobs were scarce, drove prospectors into the forests of the Abitibi region searching for opportunity.
60 miles from the Ontario border, among them was Jean-Jacques “Jack” Sullivan, whose discoveries helped ignite a gold rush that transformed the region.
5 major mines—including Sullivan, Lamaque and Sigma—helped turn a remote settlement into a thriving mining city. Roads, railways, schools and businesses followed.
Mining discoveries didn’t just produce gold. They created livelihoods, built communities and helped establish Quebec as one of the world’s great mining jurisdictions. Today, the Abitibi region remains an important centre of Canadian gold production and exploration, proof that a great mining district can deliver opportunity for generations. And somewhere in Quebec, six one-ounce gold coins await a treasure hunter who can unlock the clues hidden in this history.

Saskatchewan’s buried treasure
Not every great Canadian treasure glitters.
Some of the most valuable minerals ever discovered in this country are pink.
Beneath Saskatchewan’s seemingly endless wheat fields lie some of the world’s richest potash deposits, essential to producing the fertilizer that helps farmers feed billions of people.
Their origins stretch back more than 350 million years, when an ancient inland sea covered much of what is now Saskatchewan.
As the sea evaporated, it left behind enormous deposits of potassium-rich minerals, hidden underground for millions of years.
Developing those deposits presented engineering challenges. Water-bearing rock made sinking mine shafts difficult, requiring new techniques and persistence.
But Canadian miners and engineers found a way.
Communities such as Esterhazy, Rocanville and Lanigan grew alongside the industry as exploration pushed west on the old yellow-head trail, while Saskatchewan became a global potash powerhouse, today accounting for roughly one-third of the world’s supply.
A mineral buried beneath the Canadian Prairies helps grow food on farms from Brazil to India and China.
That’s Canadian mining making a difference on a truly global scale.
And now there’s another kind of buried treasure in Saskatchewan: six ounces of gold waiting for someone who can solve the mystery.

Manitoba’s fictional hero
Few countries have as many unusual place names as Canada. Indeed, none have a story quite like Flin Flon, a mining community named after a character in an adventure novel.
Roaming a remote northern Manitoba lake in 1914, prospector Tom Creighton discovered copper-bearing rock along the shore.
Soon it came time to name the claim, and Creighton and his partners drew inspiration from The Sunless City, a 1905 novel featuring a character named Josiah Flintabbatey Flonatin.
Thankfully, someone decided to shorten the name, and Flin Flon was born.
Generations of workers and their families were supported by the discovery, which led to a mining district that produced copper, zinc, gold and silver.
Out near Snow Lake, another important mining centre emerged. Long before that, Manitoba’s gold-mining history stretched back even earlier, including the development of the Star Lake gold mine in 1910.
Driven by early prospectors and today’s advanced mines, Manitoba’s mining history is filled with adventure, perseverance and remarkable characters.
Beyond doubt, it’s a story worthy of a novel.

Sudbury’s cosmic collision
If you think Canada’s mining history is full of extraordinary stories, consider this one.
About 1.85 billion years ago, an enormous object from space slammed into what is now northern Ontario.
The impact helped create the geological conditions responsible for one of the greatest concentrations of nickel, copper and platinum-group metals on Earth.
Fast-forward to the 1880s, when railway construction helped reveal the region’s extraordinary mineral wealth.
What followed transformed Sudbury into one of the world’s great mining centres.
Companies such as Inco and Falconbridge became internationally recognized names, while generations of miners, engineers and their families built a city whose identity became inseparable from the industry.
Sudbury’s nickel helped make the stainless steel and alloys used around the world, while its copper and other metals contributed to everything from electrical infrastructure to advanced manufacturing.
Today, the region remains central to Canada’s ambitions in critical minerals, clean technology and advanced industrial production.
And Sudbury has another success story: the transformation of a landscape once scarred by industrial activity into a global example of environmental restoration.
Few places better illustrate mining’s past, present and future.
