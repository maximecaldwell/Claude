Description of Historic Place

The Taxation Data Centre (Sudbury) is a large, low-lying three-storey Late Modernist office building, nearly-square in plan. It assumes a dramatic terraced form of dynamic and sculptural saw tooth ‘cut-outs’ at its principal façade, creating rhythmically-staggered rectilinear sections. These are echoed in undercuts at the other three corners of the building. The building is clad in light-grey textured precast concrete panels and has ribbon windows that emphasize its horizontal massing.

Heritage Value

The Taxation Data Centre (Sudbury) is a Federal Heritage Building (Recognized) because of its historical associations and architectural and environmental values.

Historical value

One of six purpose-built taxation centres constructed in the late 1970s, the Taxation Data Centre (Sudbury) illustrates the federal government’s decentralization program and the history of the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA). In response to political and economic factors such as rising unemployment, deindustrialization, and declining resource sectors, the decentralization program aimed to establish a broader federal presence across the country and provide local economic stimulus. Constructed during a period of growth for the CRA, the building accommodated an expanded workforce tasked with processing a higher volume of tax documents. The building is a very good illustration of the economic shift in Greater Sudbury in the 1970s, providing much-needed employment as the region’s economy shifted away from the declining mining industry.

Architectural value

The Taxation Data Centre (Sudbury) is a very good example of a Late Modernist building that integrates elements of Brutalism and Modern Expressionism. The saw tooth ‘cut-outs’ featured at each of the corners impart a dynamic rhythm to the building which, combined with its low height and large footprint, create a monumental appearance. Brutalist elements include the visually heavy and sculptural treatment of the overall massing through the top-heavy appearance and rhythmically staggered terraced corners, as well as its uninterrupted spans of light-grey textured precast concrete panels and ribbon windows which emphasize the horizontal massing. Late Modern influence is evident in the repetition of modular elements, use of complicated forms with a simplified material palette creating a bold silhouette, and spans of glass. The building illustrates the 1970s move towards sustainable design through its low profile, horizontal “groundscraper” form, siting, and orientation which optimize natural light penetration into the interior spaces. Its multi-level glazed entry atrium anchors the building’s highly functional layout and easy user orientation to the surrounding workspaces. Designed for processing tax returns, the building features spacious, adaptable, open rooms with peripheral circulation areas, stairs, and mechanical functions, facilitating user orientation and workflow. The design prioritizes functionality through natural light and open atrium spaces. Designed by Townend, Stefura, Baleshta, Nichols Architects in a joint venture with Page and Steele Architects, the building was influenced by Omniplan Design Group’s architectural brief for data centres.

Environmental value

The Taxation Data Centre (Sudbury) is a clean-lined boxy form rising out of a simple, attractive landscape consisting of a wide lawn, winding paved paths, intermittent groupings of trees, and ample surface parking. A slight grade change across an otherwise flat site emphasizes the building’s principal façade from Notre Dame Avenue. It is compatible with its heterogeneous suburban setting consisting of retail, office, residential buildings, and natural areas. The building is well known in Sudbury due to its size, location, architecture, and its on-going role as an important source of employment in the local economy.

Character-Defining Elements

The character-defining elements that express the heritage values of the Taxation Data Centre (Sudbury) should be respected, and include:

- its monumental, sculptural, low-lying massing;

- its terraced main façade of cascading saw-tooth ‘cut-outs’ that create rhythmically staggered modular rectilinear terraces;

- its undercut corners with saw-tooth ‘cut-outs’;

- its exterior finishes including: - its light grey precast concrete panel cladding with hammered ribs that create a recurring pattern of double and single lines;

- its exposed aggregate concrete panel system which runs along the base of the building, giving the building a top-heavy look;

- its silver aluminum window framing, which along with the slender ribbon windows, emphasize the horizontal massing;

- its multi-level glazed atrium space, which allows for abundant natural light from the south and maximizes light penetration to the interior;

- its open, interconnected interior circulation, centred on the atrium, and its flexible workspaces;

- its interior palette of soft and neutral finishes, including its oak handrails, glass balustrades, and terrazzo flooring;

- its minimal landscape design featuring lawns, trees, and pathways.
